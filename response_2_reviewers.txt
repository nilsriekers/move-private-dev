1.1 Two-stage process description is inaccurate
For related work, I would strongly suggest revising your description, starting with the paragraph that begins at line 96. While you are of course right about the strengths of your method, it's not quite right to say that "Syllable annotation in neural networks is usually performed in a two-stage process", where you mean "first segmentation, and then classification". You make it sound like this is how *all* neural network models work, which is definitely not the case. The most widely-used neural networks classify each acoustic frame (e.g., spectrogram in a time bin) to *avoid* the need to segment audio and then classify segments. Segments are only recovered by post-processing the frame labels. This is why it is confusing to cite e.g. Steinfath et al. 2021 and then say "classification is only done after the segment onset". You could point out that if you were to rely on such an approach, it would be time consuming to first generate frame-level predictions and then post-process to recover segments; maybe that's what you mean by two-step process? But I think you are referring to the two-step approach of the Schultheiss paper. In principle, there's nothing stopping you from configuring the hyperparameters of DAS or TweetyNet so you have a relatively small network, and then applying it to very small windows. You could even use a similar sliding window approach on the multi-class predictions.

For the method we develop here, there are two ways that neural network models have been applied to laboratory studies of animal acoustic communication that are most relevant. The first way consists of fitting relatively large models that can be used to segment and classify animal sounds in an off-line manner, or potentially on-line, as we propose. These are typically frame classification models (Graves 2012) like DAS (Steinfath 2021) or TweetyNet (Cohen 2022) that classify each acoustic frame (e.g., a timebin in a spectrogram, or in some cases audio samples). Classified segments can only be recovered from frame-level predictions by post-processing. These models can be seen as sound event detection models (Stowell 2022, Bioacoustics review) or voice activity detection models (e.g., Hughes Mierle 2013) applied specifically to recordings of sounds made by single acoustically-isolated individual animals. In other words, the models are used to predict sequences of sound (Kershenbaum et al 2016), not just to detect the presence or absence of sound (Kershenbaum et al 2025). More recently, a method to segment animal sounds using transformer models (cite WhisperSeg) has been proposed. However, transformers like those used as the backbones of Large Language Models are typically orders of magnitude larger than the frame classification models of Steinfath 2021 and Cohen et al 2022, and so would be challenging to use in a setting like ours, although there are ways to accelerate inference. The Temporal Convolutional-based architecture of Steinfath et al 2021, in principle, enables faster inference than the recurrent architecture of Cohen et al 2022, and this is supported by their benchmarking results.

2.1 Accuracy alone is not informative for binary classification
For table 1 (and figure 4): accuracy is not a super-informative metric when fitting binary classification models. You should check but IME you typically are in an unbalanced setting where there are far more frames of background class than foreground class, and so it's very easy to get a "high accuracy" simply by predicting 0 for every frame. This is a failure mode that neural networks can go into when you are fitting with binary cross entropy. Although your results clearly show that's not happening here, it's something to be aware of; you don't say if you do this in the methods, but you could weight the BCE loss to mitigate this effect. For the binary classifier, you probably want to compute framewise precision, recall, and F1, like in the Steinfath 2021 paper. I see in the text you give P/R/F1 scores for bird 1 only, but you don't state in the methods how you compute these metrics; I'm guessing they are in fact the framewise metrics like in the Steinfath 2021 paper?

We have added segment-level precision, recall, and F1 metrics with collar tolerances of 5, 10, 15, and 20 ms, following the approach described in vocalpy (https://vocalpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/). We use an **onset-only collar criterion**: a predicted segment is counted as a true positive when its onset falls within ±collar ms of a ground-truth syllable onset, regardless of offset timing. Bird 3 achieves lower F1 due to short inter-syllable intervals that limit the sliding window's ability to separate rapidly successive syllables; without sliding window post-processing, Bird 3 achieves onset collar F1 of 0.937, confirming accurate network-level onset detection.

As a baseline comparison, we evaluated the energy-based amplitude-threshold segmentation method (evfuncs; Nicholson, 2021) — the same method used in MooveGUI for initial segmentation — using the same onset-only collar metrics. For each bird, we performed a grid search over dB thresholds (-70 to -30 dB) on 80% of annotated files and evaluated on the remaining 20%, repeated 3 times. Results are included in Table 1 alongside the neural network metrics.

4.10 Version constraints: remove upper bounds
One other minor detail I noticed from the README and docs: it is surprising to see required versions specified as less than a certain number (e.g., torch < 2.6). Typically for library development you want a lower bound on versions. See https://snarky.ca/why-i-dont-like-semver/ (or for a very in-depth explanation, again see: https://iscinumpy.dev/post/bound-version-constraints/). I know this rule of thumb about dependencies isn't obvious (see here http://mrussek.com/2024/01/02/python-library-dependencies.html) but IME lower-bounds with no upper caps is the least painful to deal with -- most core libraries in the scientific Python stack that you depend on will try to adhere to SemVer, with the implicit guarantee that they won't make any breaking changes to the API without a major increment in the version number.
[CODE] Change version constraints in pyproject.toml: remove upper bounds (e.g., torch < 2.6), use lower bounds only (e.g., torch >= 2.0).
We have removed all upper bounds from dependency constraints (e.g., torch<=2.5.0 → torch>=2.0.0, numpy<2.0 → numpy>=1.20) and removed six unused dev-dependencies.
